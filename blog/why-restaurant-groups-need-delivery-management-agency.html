Why Multi-Location Restaurants Need a Delivery Management Agency Like Blender

If you run more than one location and DoorDash, Uber Eats, and Grubhub get handled by whoever has time that week, you are giving away customers and margin. Here is how I think about the channel, and why we built Blender to run it.

Multi-LocationDelivery StrategyUnit Economics

By Hassan Shaikh, Founder of Blender Digital · September 25, 2026

The short answer

A restaurant group needs a delivery management agency when nobody owns the channel as their main job. Without one owner, every store manager runs promos, ads, menus, and pricing their own way. Controls slip, reporting stops lining up, and margin leaks through commissions, campaign costs, and discounts that nobody is adding up. An agency like Blender gives you one consistent operator across every location and platform, focused on growing sales and on how much of each sale you actually keep.

I talk to restaurant owners every week, and the delivery conversation almost always opens the same way. “They take 30%.” “The ads don't work.” “We're only on there because we have to be.”

I understand where that comes from. I have read enough payout statements to know how quickly this channel can eat a margin. But when I dig into those accounts, I usually find the same thing: nobody is really running them. Promos were set up a year ago and never checked. Each location looks different. Nobody can say which campaign made money.

This post is for owners and operators of restaurant groups, whether that's two locations or twenty, who are trying to decide if they need outside help on delivery. I'll explain how I see the channel, where I've watched it break, and when you honestly don't need someone like us.

Who Is Actually Running Delivery at Your Restaurants?

For most restaurant groups, the honest answer is one of these:

Who runs itWhere it breaks
Each store manager handles their own storefrontEvery location ends up with different prices, promos, photos, and menus. Controls depend on who is working that week, and there is no clean way to compare locations.
A dedicated in-house delivery managerWorks when you find the right person, but it is a full salary and benefits for a role that needs deep platform knowledge. When they leave, most of what they knew leaves with them.
Nobody, really. The owner logs in when sales dip.Settings drift, promos auto-renew, error charges go undisputed, and problems get found weeks late.
An outside management agencyYou pay a management fee, so the added profit has to cover it. If it doesn't, you shouldn't keep paying.

The store-manager setup is the one I see most, and it worries me the most. Your GMs are good at running a kitchen and a floor. Studying campaign redemption reports isn't what you hired them for. So one location runs 20% off with a minimum order, another runs a BOGO on your highest-cost item, and a third still lists the dish you dropped in March. Ask “how is delivery doing across the group?” and you get five answers from five different screens.

A multi-location brand sells consistency. Your customers expect the same burger at every location. They should also get the same storefront, the same pricing logic, and the same quality of offer. And you should get one report that measures every location the same way.

2

If You Think Delivery Apps Only Exist to Take Your Money, You're Asking the Wrong Question

The commission is real. But if that's the only way you look at the channel, you'll make decisions that shrink your business: raising prices until nobody orders, turning off every promotion, treating the tablet as a necessary evil.

Here is how I see it. A strong restaurant group's online channels do two jobs: helping people discover you and getting them to buy. Most channels only do one of those.

ChannelDiscovery and purchase
Social mediaGreat for discovery. The purchase happens later and somewhere else, if the customer remembers you.
Search and Google Maps (SEO)Strong for discovery and intent. The customer still has to call, visit, or find a way to order.
Delivery marketplaces (DoorDash, Uber Eats, Grubhub)Both in the same place. A customer finds you and pays you in the same two minutes.

That is rare in marketing. There aren't many places where someone goes from never having heard of you to placing an order without leaving the app. Seen that way, the commission is the price of a channel that finds customers and closes them in one step. Your job is to make sure that price buys you something.

This doesn't mean third-party should be your only channel. I've written about how third-party and first-party ordering should work together. But treating the marketplaces as a toll you resent is how restaurants end up underinvesting in the one place their next customer is already looking.

3

Some Customers Only Buy Food on These Apps

The marketplaces aren't a side channel anymore. They're a full ecosystem with their own memberships, like DashPass and Uber One, their own rewards, and their own habits.

There is a large group of customers, and you probably know a few, who decide what to eat by opening an app. They don't search Google for tacos near them. They don't walk past your front door. They open DoorDash and choose from what's in front of them.

If you aren't on those apps, or you're there with missing photos and a 4.2 rating, you aren't in the running for that customer. Your competitor down the street is. Every order they take strengthens their ranking, which puts them in front of the next customer too.

You can't opt out of this playing field and keep the customers who live on it. Sitting it out doesn't protect your margin. It hands those orders to someone else. If your storefronts are live but quiet, start with why restaurants go invisible on DoorDash and Uber Eats.

You Have to Obsess Over Unit Economics

This is the part most owners underestimate. Delivery profitability isn't one setting you choose once. It comes from at least six levers, and they sit in different screens, on different invoices, and often with different people:

  1. Menu markup. How much higher your delivery prices are than in-store. Too low and the commission eats you. Too high and fewer people order.
  2. Commission. Your plan tier on each platform, which also affects how much visibility you get.
  3. Marketing spend. Sponsored listings and ads, charged per click or per order.
  4. Campaign costs. The discounts you fund: percent off, BOGO, free delivery, free items.
  5. The cost of the promoted item. A BOGO on your cheapest item and a BOGO on your most expensive one are very different promos. Food cost has to be part of the math.
  6. Payout ratio. The share of your sales the platform actually deposits. This is where every other lever shows up.

Each lever can look fine on its own. The damage happens when they stack: an ad and a promo on the same order, on a high-food-cost item, with a markup that was set years ago. I've seen restaurants with growing sales and a shrinking bank deposit, and nobody noticed for months because nobody was looking at all six together.

The number I watch first

Payout ratio is the closest thing to a scoreboard the platforms give you. It isn't profit, since food, packaging, and labor still come out of it, but it tells you fast whether your promo and ad decisions are working. With Haraz Coffee House, we grew sales first, then moved the average payout ratio from 46% to 53%.

What a Delivery Management Agency Does Day to Day

For a restaurant group, this is what it looks like in practice:

  • A location's sales drop. We check whether a promo expired, an item went unavailable, the rating slipped, or the store was paused during a rush, and we tell you which one it was.
  • A platform rep pitches a co-funded campaign to one of your GMs. We run the numbers before anyone opts in.
  • An error charge shows up on a payout. It gets disputed with evidence instead of quietly written off.
  • You launch a limited-time item. It goes live on every storefront the same day, at the same price, with the same photo.
  • The month ends. You get one report across every location and platform, with the same metrics and the same definitions.

None of this is glamorous. It is steady, detailed work, and it's exactly the work that gets skipped when delivery is everybody's side job.

When You Don't Need an Agency

I would rather tell you this upfront. You probably don't need us if:

  • You run one location, you have time, and you enjoy digging into the platform dashboards yourself.
  • You already have a strong in-house person who owns delivery across the group and reports on it clearly.
  • Your kitchen can't handle more volume right now. Fix prep times and accuracy first, because more orders will only expose the problem faster.

Frequently Asked Questions

Does a multi-location restaurant need a delivery management agency?

Usually, if nobody in the group owns DoorDash, Uber Eats, and Grubhub as their main job. Without a single owner, locations drift apart on pricing, promos, and menus, and reporting stops being reliable. An agency gives you one consistent operator without the cost of a full-time hire.

Should store managers run DoorDash and Uber Eats for their own locations?

Store managers should handle the in-store side: accepting orders, keeping prep times tight, and flagging outages. Pricing, promotions, ads, menu structure, and reporting should be set centrally so every location follows the same rules.

Is it worth being on DoorDash and Uber Eats if they charge a commission?

For most restaurant groups, yes, if the channel is managed for profit. Few channels let a customer discover you and order from you in the same place, and many customers only choose food through these apps.

What is payout ratio on delivery apps?

The share of your delivery sales the platform actually deposits after commissions, fees, restaurant-funded discounts, ad charges, and error adjustments. It's a useful scoreboard, but it isn't profit.
